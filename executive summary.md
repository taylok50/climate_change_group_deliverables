## Controlling Carbon: What is Carbon Pricing and What Kind of Systems Does Canada Use?

Is Canada’s carbon pricing actually cutting emissions? Carbon pricing puts a cost on greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions to combat climate change. Canada’s industrial-targeted systems come in two main types. Cap-and-trade systems limit emissions from certain sources, while facilities buy and sell permits to cover their emissions (Dubash et al. 2022). Intensity-based systems, including output-based pricing systems (OBPS), set limits per unit production for individual facilities; if limits are surpassed, they must pay or use emissions credits (Canadian Climate Institute 2024). Canadian emissions data were analyzed to determine pricing systems’ effectiveness. Figure 1 shows regional GHG emissions since 2005.

## Alberta’s Intensity-Based Carbon Pricing

Alberta’s Technology Innovation and Emissions Reduction (TIER) is an intensity-based carbon pricing system (Government of Alberta 2026). Since its implementation in 2020, there has been a negative percent decrease in total emissions. Carbon policies must also sustain competitive markets given the reliance of Alberta’s economy on high-emitting industries.

## British Columbia’s Carbon Tax and OBPS

In 2008, BC implemented the first broad fossil-fuel combustion and revenue-neutral carbon tax in North America (Fairbrother and Rhodes 2023). Incremental increases in this tax were applied until it was replaced by OBPS and the consumer carbon tax was abolished in 2025 (Government of British Columbia 2025). No significant reduction in aggregate emissions was shown through analysis, except in specific sectors, including transportation (Pretis 2022; ECCC 2026b).

## Quebec’s Cap-and-Trade System

Quebec's Système de Plafonnement et d’Échange de Droits d’Émission (SPEDE) may not be as 'speedy' at reducing CO2 as the name suggests. Although the province’s emissions are declining, there is no clear dip following this cap-and-trade system’s implementation in 2013. Despite Quebec’s GDP increasing, industrial facilities that reduced their emissions with SPEDE may have in part done so by cutting their overall economic activity (Hanoteau and Talbot 2019).

## Ontario’s Shift to Emissions Performance Standards

Ontario has had three industrial systems since 2017: cap-and-trade, federal OBPS, and now its Emissions Performance Standards (EPS) (FAO 2018; ICAP 2026). Ontario’s emissions decline primarily came from closing coal plants before industrial pricing (Office of the Auditor General of Ontario 2019). Comparing the first three years of EPS (2022-2024) with 2017-2019, emissions from steel, cement, chemicals and refining rose (ECCC 2026b). Because the system limits emissions per unit product, factories may have become cleaner while producing more.

## The Federal Output-Based Pricing System

The federal OBPS is Canada’s default system for jurisdictions without a carbon pricing system (ECCC 2026a). It applies in Manitoba, Nunavut, Prince Edward Island, and Yukon. Production-related emissions data from these regions revealed no overall trends after OBPS implementation, except reduced emissions from halocarbons, including hydrofluorocarbons (ECCC 2026b). However, this may be attributed to other regulations targeting hydrofluorocarbons, implemented the same year (ECCC 2019).

## Key Findings and Limitations

Across Canada’s various carbon pricing systems, it is uncertain whether carbon pricing cuts emissions. Studies with comparison groups found reductions in some covered sectors, such as BC’s transportation and Quebec’s regulated facilities (Pretis 2022; Hanoteau and Talbot 2019). Provincial trends were inconclusive due to limited data on recessions, the pandemic, coal phase-outs, and facility-level production. Low stringency and weak incentives may also limit results (Cui 2025). Future research should compare facilities inside and outside each system.
